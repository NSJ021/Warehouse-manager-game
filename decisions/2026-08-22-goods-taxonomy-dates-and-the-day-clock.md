# Goods taxonomy, Large orientation, dates as contract properties, and the day clock

- **Date:** 2026-08-22
- **Status:** Accepted
- **Deciders:** NJ
- **Builds on:** [storage-cells](2026-08-17-storage-cells.md) (ADR 18), [solo-drag-is-a-hold-mode](2026-08-19-solo-drag-is-a-hold-mode.md) (ADR 19), [detection-and-patch-maths](2026-08-19-detection-and-patch-maths.md) (ADR 20), [two-currencies-and-the-crew-split](2026-08-19-two-currencies-and-the-crew-split.md) (ADR 21), [orders-are-manifests-reputation-is-a-market](2026-08-19-orders-are-manifests-reputation-is-a-market.md) (ADR 22), [rack-presentation-ratified](2026-08-21-rack-presentation-ratified.md) (ADR 24).
- **Supersedes:** Nothing.
- **Amends:** `.planning/REQUIREMENTS.md` GOODS-01, GOODS-02, GOODS-03 and DMG-01; `docs/GDD.md` §6.1's "Store-until date" and "Size class" rows and §6.4's damage-source list. None of ADR 18's dimensions, atomicity or LIFO rule are touched, and ADR 24's ruling on the wall rack's back row is not reopened.

## Context

Three things forced this ADR, in the order they were found.

**Phase 1 shipped storage with exactly one kind of cargo** — `Crate.KIND_SMALL`, a single mesh with a single set of physical properties. ADR 18's atomicity rule keys a cell's occupancy off "kind", and nothing in `decisions/` has ever said what a kind actually *is*. Phase 2 cannot spawn a second cargo type, let alone ten, without answering that question first, and every plan after this one is written assuming an answer exists.

**The Phase 1 gate left one question explicitly open and routed it here.** ADR 24 ratified the rack's frame and cell presentation but could not rule on which two cells a Large occupies, because Large cargo does not exist yet — `REQUIREMENTS.md` GOODS-01 and `docs/GDD.md` §6.1 both carry the open question forward verbatim, and the roadmap names it as a Phase 2 planning input.

**GDD §5's day loop has existed since the first design session and nothing in the codebase implements any of it.** MORNING → SHIFT → CLOSE is a diagram with no clock, no door and no manifest behind it — grepped, zero matches for any of the three. The Phase 2 gate is "the loop closes" (GDD §13); that cannot be judged against a loop with no clock driving it.

A fourth thing surfaced while writing this decision rather than before it: `GOODS-03` currently reads "a store-until date that is both the deadline and the spoilage limit," and `docs/GDD.md` repeats that in two places — §6.1's property table and §6.4's damage-source list. The design discussion this ADR formalises (`.planning/phases/02-goods/02-CONTEXT.md`, NJ, 2026-08-21) explicitly rejected an independent spoilage timer. Building Phase 2 against the requirement as currently worded would add a degradation mechanic NJ has ruled out; building it against the design discussion while the requirement still says otherwise would leave the project with two contradictory sources of truth in `decisions/` and `.planning/`, which is exactly the failure mode [gsd-wraps-the-build-order](2026-08-17-gsd-wraps-the-build-order.md) (ADR 15) exists to prevent. This ADR resolves that contradiction explicitly rather than letting a later plan silently pick a side.

## Decision

### (a) Two tiers: categories carry the mechanics, variants carry the comedy

Cargo has roughly ten mechanical **categories** — powders, textiles, glassware, masonry (bricks and the like), tinned goods, electronics, machine parts, white goods, novelty items, and a dodgy-goods category of its own (see (b)). A category is the thing ADR 18 has been calling a "kind" all along: it fixes a weight band, a fragility rating (0–3), a value density, the plaque text a cell shows, and — the load-bearing part — **cell atomicity is defined at category level**, not below it.

Underneath each category sit many **variants**: manifest names and crate decals, and nothing else. A powders category variant might be flour, plaster of Paris or talcum powder; none of that changes how the game treats the crate. This is deliberately the cheap axis. Putting any mechanic on a variant — its own weight, its own fragility — would multiply the balancing surface by the content budget, so every new joke name written for a manifest would also be a new thing to tune. Ten categories is a number that can be balanced by hand; forty mechanically-distinct types is not, and the taxonomy exists specifically so content can scale without that cost.

Two consequences follow directly and are worth stating because they change how a cell behaves. **Mixing variants of the same category inside one cell is legal** — a cell of tinned goods can hold tinned peaches on top of tinned soup, because atomicity only cares that both are "tinned goods." LIFO digging past a variant you didn't want is therefore a feature this ADR intends, not an edge case 02-05 has to guard against. And **comping is like-for-like at category level, not variant level**, which is what keeps ADR 22's third fork — comp a replacement from what the building already contains — alive at any variety of manifest names. "Ordered porcelain ducks, comped porcelain badgers" is legal under this ADR, and it is funny precisely because it is legal.

### (b) Dodgy goods are a self-labelled category, not a system

One category is dodgy goods, and it announces itself: names that protest too much — "100% Baby Talcum Powder", "Definately Legal Tobacco" (the misspelling is deliberate and must survive a spellcheck pass, because catching it is not the joke), "Bundles of Used Fivers." There is no contraband mechanic, no hidden-goods detection, nothing that inspects a crate's contents and decides whether it is legal. GDD §6.6 already promises exactly this posture for client personalities — dodgy clients "played entirely through trust and suspicion... no contraband mechanic and no police" — and this clause is the goods-side half of the same joke, not a new system next to it. It pairs directly with ADR 22's reputation split: dodgy damage is punished in cash, and cash does not decay with the lease, so a dodgy client's crate is exactly as risky on day 29 as it is on day 1.

### (c) Weight is per-category and deliberately deceptive

Weight bands are set per category, and they are not sorted to be predictable. Glass is heavy, glass is heavy, glass is heavy, textiles are light, bricks are heavy enough to need two people every time. **Heavy Smalls are legal and wanted, not an oversight**: a Small that exceeds the solo-lift limit is dragged whether or not anyone asked for it — ADR 19's existing drag mechanism, unchanged — and because a dragged item can only be left at floor level, a heavy Small sitting on the floor is itself an advertisement for two-player carry. Once a player has learned that "glassware is always heavy," the category becomes readable at a glance; the surprise lives in a mixed delivery row where the categories are jumbled, not in any single item once its category is known. That is the intended learning curve, and softening the weights to make them intuitive on first contact would remove the joke rather than fix a bug.

This closes a question ADR 19 explicitly left open when it was written, because Large cargo did not exist yet: "the intent is that Large is the only thing a solo player cannot lift. Revisit when Large exists." **Every Large exceeds the solo-lift limit, always** — this is that revisit, and it is now a hard rule rather than a mass number waiting to be calibrated against a size class that did not exist. What this ADR deliberately does **not** fix is Medium: **a Medium is decided by weight, not by size.** A light Medium — a box of feathers the size of a washing machine — is a normal one-handed carry despite occluding your view exactly as GDD §6.1 already describes; a heavy Medium behaves like a heavy Small, dragged or two-player-carried. Size and weight are independent axes on purpose, because collapsing them would make weight predictable from a glance at the box's silhouette, and the whole point of (c) is that it isn't.

### (d) A Large takes two cells, and the player picks which two

This answers the question the Phase 1 gate carried forward. A Large occupies two adjacent cells, in **either** orientation: side-by-side across a rack's face, with the plaque centred across both, or front-to-back through the rack's own depth. The player chooses at placement time; nothing about the cargo itself fixes an orientation. NJ overrode the earlier side-by-side-only lean specifically because the situational choice — which orientation fits the gap you actually have — is the strategy, not a UI convenience.

Front-to-back placement anchors at the aimable **front** cell of the pair and extends logically into the cell behind it. That means a wall rack's permanently-buried back row, ratified as a level-design property in ADR 24, becomes usable by Larges **with no change to the aim scheme at all** — the front cell is still what a raycast resolves, exactly as it does today. Say this plainly because it would be easy to misread otherwise: this does **not** reopen ADR 24. ADR 24 ruled the wall rack's back row a level-design property, reachable through the rack's end faces, and declined to treat it as a bug needing a fix. Front-to-back Large placement is a bonus that falls out of the existing geometry, not a patch for a problem ADR 24 chose to leave alone. Whichever orientation is chosen, the pair is recorded as **one id spanning two cells** — there is one crate record, one condition, one place in the round-trip invariant STORE-07 is building toward, not two half-records that have to stay in sync.

This decision has a real UX cost, named here rather than discovered later: a rotate control, cycling a ghost preview between the orientations that actually fit the gap the player is aiming at (and refusing, visibly, the ones that don't). A rotate control with nothing showing what it rotates *into* is unusable, so the ghost preview — queued in `.planning/STATE.md`'s open items as Phase 6 scope — is promoted into Phase 2 as a direct consequence of building both orientations. That promotion is bounded deliberately: the ghost replaces the glow cube **for Large placement only**. The cell highlight keeps doing its existing job for retrieval and for Small/Medium placement, and the fuller Phase 6 aim-feedback rework — grab-target reticles, red-on-full-cell visibility, the colour-blind channel — is not brought forward wholesale just because one of its pieces had to arrive early.

### (e) Store-until dates are contract properties, not kind properties

This is the clause that amends a requirement, and it is worth being explicit about which one: `GOODS-03` currently reads "a store-until date that is both the deadline and the spoilage limit." It is now only the first half of that sentence. A store-until date says when a client is due to collect a crate. There is **no independent spoilage timer and no degradation over time** — a crate stored for a day and the same crate stored for a fortnight are in identical condition, provided nothing physical happened to either of them in between. Only physics changes condition; the calendar does not.

The reasoning is the strongest part of this clause and is worth stating rather than asserting. A spoilage timer creates damage the players did not cause. That is precisely the opposite of P3 — clumsiness is the comedy — and it would put a source of condition loss entirely outside the dilemma the whole game is built on: patch, confess or comp only make sense as choices about something a player *did*. A crate that arrives Destroyed because a real-time clock ran out while nobody touched it turns the pillar into an alarm clock, not a decision.

Two lateness cases follow, and they are deliberately routed differently:

**"You're late"** — a collection is missed at the door going down for the night. The consequence forks exactly as ADR 22 already describes: legit clients punish in reputation, dodgy ones in cash, part-fulfilment falls out of the crate-as-unit-of-handover rule for free, and comping stays like-for-like. None of that is new; what is new is the cost of redelivery, which is **a locked row on tomorrow's manifest** — an opportunity cost, not a fine, because it is a slot that could have gone to a fresh order and didn't. Phase 2 builds the date itself, the door-down check that notices a miss, and that locked-row cost. The reputation and cash forks stay Phase 3/4 work, wired once damage and the client system exist to receive them.

**"They're late"** — a client fails to collect at all, past their own date. The primary cost here needs no new system: it is **emergent**. An overdue crate keeps occupying a cell, and because tomorrow's manifest is capacity-gated, overstaying stock quietly starves tomorrow's options. On top of that sits a small, deliberately unstrategic **demurrage income** — a per-overdue-day trickle, shown as its own smug tally column in the spirit of ADR 21's contribution columns, never sized to matter as a plan.

**The plaque date turns red once it is overdue.** This is the one presentation clause in this ADR, and it belongs here rather than being left implicit, because it makes per-cell signage — GDD §6.3, recommended at the Phase 1 gate and ratified as an anchor point in ADR 24 — a **requirement** of this decision rather than a nice-to-have: a store-until date that can go overdue has to be visible somewhere, or "being late costs something" (the Phase 2 gate's own fourth success criterion) has no way to read as true.

One idea was considered and parked rather than silently dropped: "claim as done," a mechanism for writing off overdue stock without a client ever collecting it, was raised in the design discussion and routed to `docs/idea-book.md` as sales-counter strand 6 (commit `f923a8d`). It needs its own superseding ADR before it enters scope, and this one does not build any part of it.

### (f) The roller door is the day clock, and it governs commerce, not people

The day loop gets its clock. A roller door is the visible mechanism: a klaxon warns, the door opens and closes **on time**, and trucks and clients live entirely by that schedule. **Players always have a side way in and out, and are never locked in or out by the door itself.** State plainly why: locking a player out of their own warehouse is a punishment this game has never chosen to make anywhere else, and a door that traps someone is a bug report waiting to happen, not a mechanic worth defending.

**Morning** opens with the door going up and a truck dumping the day's delivery as a roughly 30-second ceremony before the working day starts. That dump needs a ceiling, and the ceiling has to hold on two different axes at once, not one.

The first axis is the physics budget (ADR 14): a body count. A Large is one rigid body, exactly like a Small — the ~150-body envelope is measured per body, at roughly 40 microseconds a crate whether it moves or sleeps, and does not care what the body contains. A delivery's **body cap** guards that envelope directly, and it is deliberately size-blind, because the physics engine is.

The second axis is storage volume, and it is a genuinely different number from the first. Per `storage_grid.gd`, a cell holds 8 Smalls, 1 Medium or half a Large — so in cell-equivalents a Small is ⅛ of a cell, a Medium is 1, a Large is 2, a 16× spread from smallest to largest. A body cap cannot see that spread: 24 Smalls is 3 cells of stock, 24 Larges is 48 cells and will not fit most rooms this phase builds. So the delivery also carries a **cell-equivalent volume cap**. This mirrors ADR 18's own precedent directly and is worth citing rather than reinventing: storage fees price volume, never items, because a single crate count makes Smalls strictly dominant and collapses the packing decision — a delivery ceiling that only counted crates would make exactly that mistake at the door instead of at the till.

On top of both caps sit **per-size composition limits** — a ceiling on how many Larges, and separately how many Mediums, one day's delivery may contain. This is NJ's own addition, not implied by the other two: nothing about a body cap or a volume cap rules out an absurd delivery that is technically legal by both measures, and the composition limit exists to stop that mix being authored at all, as its own rule rather than an emergent property.

Every one of these caps — body count, cell-equivalent volume, per-size limits — is an **exported tunable, not a number this ADR fixes**, for the same reason day length gets a few paragraphs below: changing a constant and a test bound costs minutes, changing a number written into an append-only ADR costs a superseding one. **Crew size is named here as the axis these caps eventually scale against** — a four-player crew can absorb a bigger delivery than a solo player can, and that scaling is where SOLO-01's own tuning will eventually live; it is not solved by this ADR.

What the caps are tuned toward outlives any particular number, so it is worth recording in NJ's own terms, because it gives the Phase 2 gate something real to judge a delivery against rather than a number that might simply have felt right once. A morning delivery must **feel worthy** — a substantial event, not a trickle; it must **feel deliberate** — authored, not random noise; it must **feel achievable** — a crew can actually clear it inside the day; and it must **feel like earning your pay** — the work is effortful, and the effort is the point. The player-minutes arithmetic bounds the third of those and is the guard against the first being set too timidly: the Phase 1 gate timed a relaxed intake → rack → outbound loop at just under a minute per crate, so an 8-minute day gives a four-player crew doing nothing else roughly 32 crate-handlings, and a solo player roughly 8 — the frame any future tuning of these caps has to fit inside.

**The day is a full real-time frame, and open hours are a subset of it** — roughly two-thirds of the day is open trading, the remaining third is after-hours tidy-up time with the door shut to new business. After-hours is a **skippable cap**, not a mandatory phase: the host can call it a night early and go straight to the midnight ceremony, exactly the host-decides-unilaterally precedent ADR 21 already establishes for the checkout split — no vote, no confirmation from the rest of the crew.

**Day length stays an exported tunable, and this ADR does not fix a number.** Say that plainly, because it would be easy to assume otherwise given everything else here is specific: NJ has not agreed a figure. GDD RUN-02's 6–10 minute target bounds the default, and the real constraint is lease arithmetic — a 10-day or 30-day term (ADR 8) multiplied by whatever the day length turns out to be has to add up to a session length that still makes sense as "a sprint" or "the core game" respectively.

**The midnight ceremony is the day boundary and the v1 join window, and it will be the save point once saving is built.** It pauses play, shows the day's tally, and hands over tomorrow's manifest. The day boundary (02-03's clock, 02-10's midnight summary and day roll) and the v1 join window (late-joiner catch-up across 02-03's clock state, 02-07's manifest and 02-10's summary) are both genuinely built by this phase. The save point is not, and this ADR does not pretend otherwise: **Phase 5 (the run) owns it**, because that is where the Lease Run wrapper lives, and a 30-day term at 6–10 minutes a day is long enough that saving stops being optional. Phase 2 builds no persistence of any kind.

**Money, rent and the offer sheet are Phase 4's work, not this ADR's.** Phase 2 ships **scripted days** — a hand-authored stand-in for the day's manifest — so the loop can be judged as closing without an economy behind it yet. The scripted day's data shape and the eventual offer sheet's output shape are deliberately the same shape, so that Phase 4 replaces the author of the day's content, not the contract the rest of the game reads it through.

**Phone events are Phase 4** as well, and are not part of this ADR's scope.

One principle is stated here because this is the first system it binds, and it will bind every system after it: this project assumes open voice comms between players (Discord, or the in-game proximity voice ADR 9 already commits to). **Information asymmetry must live on the screen, never in audio.** A klaxon nobody happens to be near when it sounds must never be the only warning that the door is closing — if a warning matters, it has a screen-readable form as well as a sound. Proximity voice remains comedy and atmosphere layered on top of the game, never load-bearing for it.

## Consequences

**Easier:** ADR 18's atomicity rule finally has a definition of "kind" to key off. Content scales on the cheap axis — new manifest names and decals cost nothing to balance because they carry no mechanics. A wall rack's dead back row gets a real use instead of staying a level-design curiosity. The day loop stops being a diagram in GDD §5 and becomes something with a clock, a door and a ceremony behind it.

**Harder — the honest cost:** the rack's occupancy model is genuinely Small-shaped today. `Rack.can_accept`, `add_to_cell`, `apply_cell_filled`, `occupancy_snapshot` and `occupied_cells_in_top_row` all assume a homogeneous eight-slot stack of one crate type, and this ADR is a redesign of that model — categories, variants, weight-gated hold modes, a two-cell record for Large — not a size parameter bolted onto the existing one. Large's rotate control and ghost preview are also real UX surface where there was previously one keypress and one glow cube, and that surface has to be built in Phase 2 rather than deferred with everything else in the Phase 6 aim-feedback queue.

**Watch for:** the weight deception (c) being read as a bug the first time a player cannot lift a Small they expected to — the correct fix, always, is a plaque and a legible weight tell, never quietly softening the weight bands themselves, or the joke (c) exists to make disappears. Also watch value bands: they are set to sit inside ADR 20's £50–£2000 sweep envelope, and `VALUE_REFERENCE = 2000` is a real ceiling past which the detection curve's value term saturates — a 30-day lease's higher-value cargo mix is already flagged as a Phase 4 re-check, not solved here.

**Rules out:** an independent spoilage timer or any condition degradation that is not caused by something a player did; per-variant mechanics of any kind; a Large that fits in one cell; a store-until date that is anything other than a contract property; a door that locks a player in or out of their own warehouse; a delivery ceiling fixed as a literal number in this ADR rather than as exported tunables settled in play; and any information asymmetry that exists only in audio and not on the screen.

## Alternatives considered

**One flat list of roughly forty cargo types, with no category tier above them.** Simpler to author — every type is just itself, with no indirection. Rejected because every type would then carry its own weight, fragility and value balance, and because atomicity would fragment cells so finely that a mixed delivery could never be sensibly packed into one cell at all.

**Side-by-side orientation only for Large cargo**, the earlier lean before this design pass. Simpler to build — one footprint, no rotate control, no ghost preview needed in Phase 2. Rejected on two grounds: the situational choice between orientations *is* the strategic content this clause is meant to add, and locking Large to one orientation would leave a wall rack's back row permanently dead in every case where front-to-back would have made it usable for free.

**A real spoilage timer**, exactly as `docs/GDD.md` has described since its first draft and as `GOODS-03` still reads before this ADR. Straightforwardly implementable, and already written down. Rejected on P3 grounds — see (e): it creates damage nobody caused, which sits outside the dilemma rather than feeding it.

**The day clock as an autoload**, the obvious place to put anything that needs to be reachable from everywhere. Rejected by `docs/project-structure.md`'s own reasoning, which names this exact temptation by phase number ahead of time: the day clock is per-run state reachable from the level, not global infrastructure in the shape `Net` is, and making it an autoload would break the `--script` unit runner for any pure module that ever needed to touch it.

**Fixing the morning delivery ceiling as a literal number here**, consistent with how firmly this ADR states most of its other figures. Rejected for this one number specifically: a delivery cap is the number in the whole day loop most likely to need moving after a single playtest, changing an exported constant and a test sweep costs minutes, and changing a number embedded in an append-only ADR costs a superseding one — the identical reasoning clause (f) already applies to day length, applied here to the same clause's other unfixed number.
